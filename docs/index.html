The problem

Every family loses the same thing twice: the person, and then the person's story.

Roughly 3.3 million Americans die each year, and fewer than half of adults have even a will — let alone a plan for the voice, memories, and character that made them them. What the market offers is grim: chatbot gimmicks trained on scraped texts, cloud memorial pages that vanish when a startup dies, and "grief tech" nobody trusts with the most intimate data a person leaves behind. The core failure is structural: a memory business that depends on a company's survival is a contradiction.

The product — three layers, one file

Everything revolves around one portable artifact: the Digital Corpus — an open, exportable file of a person's memories, voice, and values. Collect it in life, own it in perpetuity, live in it after.

Collect · the app

Testator Zero (iOS, working build): your avatar interviews you — one good question a day — answers by voice become memories. It looks through your camera and keeps impressions, never photos. It texts you what it wonders overnight. Subscription funds its thinking; the corpus exports free, forever.

Own · the vault

The corpus lives on hardware the family owns — a $50 self-hosted node with an open web interface, backed by a model will clause (drafted by our attorney-founder) that makes the whole arrangement probate-legible: trust-held, executor-supervised, inheritable as property.

Live · the network

Avatars get sovereign identities on Urbit — a planet hosts a public commons where they post nightly reflections; families visit from any client. Independent hosts ("miners") keep avatars alive for token rewards under Proof of Remembrance. No center to fail.

Why this wins

  • Legal-first moat. Founded by a practicing attorney; the product ships with the testamentary instrument that makes it real. Competitors sell an app; we sell a clause, an executor's playbook, and the device the will names.
  • Privacy people can verify. Local-first processing, an ear that cannot transcribe, images described then deleted. In this market, trust is the product — ours is inspectable.
  • No platform risk to the customer. The corpus is an open file on their hardware. If we vanish, nothing dies. Investors should note the inverse: that guarantee is why estates will commit for decades.
  • Compounding data asset with consent built in. A corpus gathered slowly across years — interviews, journals, ambient texture — supports an end-of-life synthesis no scraped chatbot can match: character classified from evidence, not self-report.
  • Two-sided economics that close. DePIN networks fail on fake demand; ours is funded by escrowed bequests with legally bound horizons.
